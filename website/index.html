From neurons to next steps

At Pragnya’s outings for neurodivergent kids and adults, and in everyday schoolwork, the same instruction can land one moment and be lost the next. That raised a question: does what a brain is doing change what it takes in? We tested it in two public brain recordings, tried hard to break each answer, and turned what held up into the design of two apps.

One loop, five chapters

It starts in real life and ends back there. Each chapter answers the question the last one raised.

  1. 1 · Real life

    Same words, different moment.

    At Pragnya’s outings and in schoolwork, a step that is easy when someone is calm can be lost when they are tired, rushed or overloaded. Is that the brain itself?

    To find out: look inside a brain while its state changes.

  2. 2 · Understand

    A mouse’s state shapes what its visual cortex does.

    Thousands of neurons, recorded while mice watched pictures. We predicted each neuron from the picture alone, then from the picture plus pupil size and running.

    Found: knowing the mouse’s state made prediction 25–34% better, in all three mice. Read chapter 2 →

  3. 3 · Measure

    Can a sensor read state in a person?

    Eyes closed is one of the clearest changes in brain waves. One person, 14 scalp sensors, 117 seconds, and the model is always tested on what comes later.

    Found: on one stretch, yes (0.625, or 0.728 with four glitches limited). Across the recording it drifted to 0.478, about a coin flip. Read chapter 3 →

  4. 4 · Design

    From findings to features.

    If state matters but cannot be read from outside, an app should ask, not guess. It should show less on a low-energy day, and make pausing and coming back easy.

    Built into: Next Step, by Amishi Mehta, for schoolwork, and Helper Jobs, a family project, for real jobs at Pragnya outings. Read chapter 4 →

  5. 5 · Test, then go further

    Where this goes next.

    Four more data experiments, design studies with Pragnya volunteers and families, and papers others can check.

    The aim: one research direction, where neuroscience and design meet. Read chapter 5 →

The loop: real life raised the question; the mouse showed that state changes what a brain takes in; the EEG showed that state is hard to read from outside; so the apps ask how someone is doing and keep each step small. The last experiment takes the apps back to real life to see if they help. Three mice and one person are a start, not a rule for everyone.

What this does not show yet: whether the pattern holds in more animals and more people, what causes it, or whether the apps help. Each of those is an experiment in chapter 5.

How we tried to break each result

Nothing in the story counts until it has survived a check that could have made it disappear.

  • Shuffle the answers

    A coin flip, as it should be

    With the EEG labels scrambled 20 times, the score falls to 0.499. Mouse behaviour shuffled 20 times adds almost nothing.

  • Split by time

    Train on the past, test the future

    No random mixing of neighbouring moments, which makes EEG results look far better than they are.

  • Leave a gap

    90 samples between train and test

    Removing the moments right next to each test block keeps the EEG result near chance: it is not leakage.

  • Leave one out

    No single mouse carries it

    The average gain stays positive (+0.040 to +0.048) whichever mouse is left out.

  • Glitch check

    Four broken samples found

    Values up to 715,897 where normal is about 4,000. Limiting them helps the last block, not the drift.

  • Every number traced

    Rebuilt from saved results

    The charts are generated from the committed results files, and the EEG study reruns with one command.

Who did what

The studies were done by the applicant with a parent collaborator, who helped scope questions, review code and debug. Software tools, including AI assistants, helped build this website. The contribution ledger lists each part.

What it is not

It is not a clinical or diagnostic study. Three mice and one EEG recording cannot speak for all animals or people, and a model that predicts something does not show what causes it.
