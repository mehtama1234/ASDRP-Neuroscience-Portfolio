What we found, in three sentences

The question: does how alert you are change what your brain takes in, and can a device measure it? Each answer links to its evidence.

  1. 1 · The mouse study

    In mice, brain cells reacted differently to the very same picture depending on how alert the mouse was.

    We used public recordings of thousands of brain cells in three mice. Adding how alert each mouse was (its pupil size and running speed) made our predictions of the cells 25–33% better, in all three.

    The mouse study →

  2. 2 · The brain-wave study

    A brain-wave headset on a person could not reliably tell even whether their eyes were open or closed.

    We used a public recording of one person. A computer learned to spot closed eyes from the brain waves, but within two minutes it was no better than flipping a coin, even with a smarter method.

    The EEG study →

  3. 3 · The app

    So a helpful app should not try to read your brain: it should ask how you feel and show one small step at a time.

    Next Step, Amishi’s app for students, asks “How much energy do you have today?” On a low day it shows only the next step, so nothing has to guess.

    The design →

The big picture

Instructions, apps and classrooms often assume a brain is always ready to take things in. It is not.

So this work asks three questions in order: how much does a brain’s state (how alert, tired or calm it is) change what it takes in, can a device measure that state, and how do we build tools that work either way? Then it checks whether those tools actually help.

  • Understand

    How much does alertness change what the brain takes in?

    A mouse study and a brain-wave (EEG) study. Each was tested on recordings the computer had not seen, and checked in ways that could have proved it wrong.

  • Design

    Tools that work on good days and bad days

    Ask how someone is doing instead of guessing, show one step at a time, and make pausing easy.

  • Help

    With the people they are for

    Next Step for students and Helper Jobs for Pragnya’s Sunday outings, tested with the people who use them next.

Where it leads: a research path that uses brain science to design apps that work for real people.

One loop, five chapters

It starts in real life and ends back there. Each chapter answers the question the last one raised.

  1. 1 · Real life

    Same words, different moment.

    It started from Amishi’s own experience: a step that is easy when you are calm can be lost when you are tired, rushed or overloaded. Is that the brain itself?

    To find out: look inside a brain while its state changes.

  2. 2 · Understand

    How alert a mouse is changes how its brain responds to what it sees.

    Mice watched pictures while thousands of brain cells were recorded. We guessed what each cell would do from the picture alone, then from the picture plus how alert and active the mouse was.

    Found: knowing the mouse’s state made our guesses 25–33% better, in all three mice. Read chapter 2 →

  3. 3 · Measure

    Can a brain-wave headset tell when a person’s eyes are closed?

    A cap of 14 sensors on one person’s head for two minutes (EEG). Closing your eyes is one of the clearest changes it picks up. Can a simple model tell when?

    Found: on one stretch it scored 0.625 (0.5 is a coin flip, 1 is always right). Across the whole recording it fell to 0.478, about a coin flip. A smarter method using brain-wave rhythms scored 0.566, but still fell below a coin flip on some stretches. Read chapter 3 →

  4. 4 · Design

    From findings to features.

    If alertness matters but a headset cannot reliably measure it, an app should ask, not guess. It should show less on a low-energy day, and make pausing and coming back easy.

    Built into: Next Step, by Amishi Mehta, for schoolwork, and Helper Jobs, a family project that gives neurodivergent adults one picture step at a time while they help at Pragnya’s Sunday outings for kids. Read chapter 4 →

  5. 5 · Test, then go further

    Where this goes next.

    One follow-up experiment is done. Next: three more experiments on the same public data, testing the apps with Pragnya volunteers and families, and write-ups others can check.

    The aim: a research path that uses brain science to design apps that work for real people. Read chapter 5 →

In short: how alert you are changes what your brain takes in, a headset could not reliably measure it, so a good app asks and keeps steps small. Three mice and one person are a start, not a rule for everyone.

What this does not show yet: whether the pattern holds in more animals and more people, what causes it, or whether the apps help. Each of those is an experiment in chapter 5.

How we tried to break each result

Nothing in the story counts until it has survived a check that could have made it disappear.

  • Mix up the answers

    It should fail, and it does

    If we scramble which moments were eyes-closed, the model drops to a coin flip. If we scramble the mouse’s state, the gain disappears. So the results come from the recordings, not from chance.

  • Never peek at the future

    Learn from earlier, test on later

    The model only ever learns from the past and is tested on what comes after, the way a real app would have to work.

  • Leave a gap

    No help from next-door moments

    Moments right next to each other look alike. We leave a gap between what the model learns from and what it is tested on.

  • Leave one mouse out

    No single mouse carries it

    Take any one mouse away and the other two still show the gain.

  • Look for glitches

    Four found and trimmed

    Four sensor readings were glitches, up to 180 times bigger than normal. Trimming them helped one test, but the computer still lost track over time.

  • Every number traced

    Rebuilt from saved results

    Every chart is drawn from the saved results files, and the EEG study reruns with one command.

Who did what

The studies were done by Amishi with her father, who helped shape the questions, review the code and fix problems. Software tools, including AI assistants, helped build this website and the apps. The contribution ledger lists each part.

What it is not

It is not a medical study and it does not diagnose anyone. Three mice and one person cannot speak for everyone, and being able to predict something is not the same as knowing what causes it.
