Chapter 3 · Measure · EEG study

Can sensors on the head tell when someone’s eyes are closed?

EEG is a cap of small sensors that pick up the brain’s electrical activity through the scalp. Closing your eyes is one of the clearest changes it can see. If a simple model cannot read even that reliably for two minutes, quieter states like tiredness will be harder still.

On one stretch, yes. Over time, no. On the last part of the recording the model scored 0.625, and 0.728 once four broken readings were capped. But tested on five stretches across the recording, it averaged 0.478: about a coin flip. Letting it see the last few moments as well did not help.

How we tested it

Learn from earlier in the recording, then test on what comes later, like a real app would have to.

  • The recording

    One person, two minutes

    14 sensors, about 128 readings a second. Each moment is marked eyes open or eyes closed (closed about 45% of the time).

  • Learn, then test

    Earlier, then later

    The model learns from the first 70% of the recording, is checked on the next 15%, and is tested once on the last 15%.

  • Two models

    Now, or now plus a moment ago

    One sees a single moment. The other also sees the last 9 readings, about a fourteenth of a second.

  • The score

    0.5 is a coin flip

    How often the model ranks an eyes-closed moment above an eyes-open one. 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is always right.

  • Five stretches

    Moving forward in time

    Learn from everything before a stretch, test on that stretch, and repeat five times across the recording.

  • The data

    A public recording

    UCI EEG Eye State (Roesler, 2013), shared for anyone to study.

The results

How to read the score

Where the results sit between guessing and always right.

Anything near 0.5 is no better than flipping a coin. Below 0.5 means the model is wrong more often than right, so the pattern it learned has flipped.

The score is called AUC.

The last stretch

Good on one stretch, better once broken readings are capped.

Four readings were broken: up to 715,897 when normal is about 4,000. Capping each sensor at the range seen while learning lifts the score from 0.625 to 0.728.

We added this check after the first results, so the new numbers sit next to the old ones, not in place of them.

Five stretches in time

Across the recording, it drifts to a coin flip.

Scores land on both sides of 0.5, and some fall well below it. Being worse than guessing means the pattern the model learned earlier had flipped by later on.

So the signal drifts within two minutes. Capping broken readings and leaving a gap do not fix it.

Making sure it is not our mistake

Three checks that the result comes from the recording, not from how we handled it.

  • Mix up the answers

    Scores 0.499, a coin flip

    With eyes open and closed scrambled 20 times, the model scores a coin flip, as it should. Our method does not invent a pattern.

  • Leave a gap

    Scores 0.448, still near guessing

    We left out the moments just before each test stretch, so the model could not lean on near-copies. The result stays near a coin flip.

  • Cap broken readings

    Four readings, capped fairly

    The caps come only from the part the model learns from, so the test part never shapes them.

How far back to look

Looking further back seems to help, but we did not pick a winner.

We chose to try 3, 9, 30 and 90 readings back before seeing any results. Ninety does best on average, but picking it now would mean tuning to this one recording.

Next: compare these on new recordings before choosing.

Experiment 2: would brain-wave bands hold steady?

Alpha waves grow when eyes close. So we asked a follow-up question, wrote the plan down before running it, and tested it the same way.

  • The idea

    Strength over the last second

    Instead of the raw reading at one moment, the model sees how strong four kinds of brain wave were over the last second, including alpha.

  • The test

    Same five stretches, plus a gap

    Learn from earlier, test on later, with one second left out between them, because the one-second windows overlap.

  • The rule set beforehand

    Better on average, never below 0.5

    It counts as holding up only if it beats the raw readings on average and no stretch falls below a coin flip.

Better on average, but it still did not hold up. All four bands averaged 0.566 across the five stretches, against 0.481 for the raw readings. But two stretches still dipped just below a coin flip (0.492 and 0.493), so by the rule we set beforehand it did not hold up. Alpha alone did worse (0.442). Mixing up the answers gave 0.500, as it should.

Experiment 2

Brain-wave bands help, but not enough to rely on.

Bands lift the average and the worst stretch, but a model that is sometimes worse than guessing is still not something to build an app on. This is why the apps ask the person.

With a one-second gap between learning and testing. Without the gap: 0.571 for bands, 0.529 for raw readings. Plan written before running: EXPERIMENT-2-PLAN.md in the source code.

Rerun it

python3 projects/eeg-eye-state/src/analyze.py writes results/metrics.json, src/analyze_bands.py writes results/bands.json for experiment 2, and python3 website/build_results.py turns it into these charts. See the full report. Data: Roesler, O. (2013). EEG Eye State. UCI Machine Learning Repository, doi:10.24432/C57G7J.

What it does not show

One person and one short recording cannot show how this works for other people. The only thing measured is eyes open or closed, not attention, tiredness or any condition.

What chapter 3 tells us: a sensor that is right on one stretch and wrong on the next, even using brain-wave bands, is not something to build a tool on. So the apps ask the person instead of guessing. Chapter 4 →
