Chapter 2 · Understand · Mouse study

Does how alert a mouse is change how its brain responds to what it sees?

Mice watched pictures while scientists recorded thousands of brain cells in the part of the brain that handles seeing. We tried to guess what each cell would do, first from the picture alone, then from the picture plus two signs of the mouse’s state: how wide its pupils were and how fast it was running. If state did not matter, the extra signs would not help.

It mattered, in all three mice. Knowing the mouse’s state made our guesses a quarter to a third better (25–33%), on pictures the model had never seen. This shows state and seeing are linked. It does not yet show which causes which.

How we tested it

Two guessers, the same pictures, and a fair test on pictures neither had seen.

  • Step 1

    What the mouse saw

    Each picture is shrunk to a short summary of its light and dark patterns, so a simple model can use it.

  • Step 2

    What state it was in

    Pupil size (wider usually means more alert) and running speed, recorded at the same moment as the brain cells.

  • Step 3

    Two simple guessers

    Guesser A sees only the picture. Guesser B sees the picture plus pupil and running. Both are the simplest kind of model: each clue gets a weight, and the weights are added up.

  • Step 4

    A fair test

    Both guess how each brain cell responds to pictures they have never seen. We score how closely each guess follows the real cell.

  • The data

    A public dataset

    Sensorium 2022: recordings of thousands of brain cells from mice watching pictures, shared by scientists for anyone to study.

  • Being fair

    Each mouse counts once

    The many brain cells in one mouse are not counted as many mice.

The results

How to read the score

How closely the guess follows the real brain cell.

0 means the guess and the cell have nothing in common; 1 would be a perfect match. Brain cells are noisy, so even good guesses score low. What matters is the jump from A to B.

The score is a correlation, averaged over every recorded brain cell.

Result

Every mouse is easier to predict once we know its state.

The picture explains part of what the seeing part of the brain does. Whether the mouse is alert and moving explains more, in every animal.

Tested on pictures the guessers never saw.

How much state adds

Every mouse gains, by different amounts.

Pupil size helps all three mice. How much running adds changes from mouse to mouse, so each mouse is shown on its own.

Gain = guesser B’s score minus guesser A’s score.

Which part of state?

It is the pupil, not the running.

Adding running barely moved the score. Adding pupil size gave almost the whole gain. This mouse ran on only about 5% of trials, so running had little to say.

Score on unseen pictures, first mouse.

Which cells gain?

The cells that follow alertness gain most.

Cells whose activity rises or falls with pupil size gained about five times as much as the rest.

A cell “follows pupil” when its activity and pupil size move together (correlation above 0.1), as in Amishi’s report.

One cell

From nearly unpredictable to predictable.

Brain cell 3035 was almost impossible to predict from the picture alone. Knowing the mouse’s state raised its score from 0.05 to 0.40.

Trying to break the result

Three checks that could have made the gain disappear. It survived all three.

  • Mix up the state

    The gain disappears

    We gave guesser B the pupil and running from the wrong moments, 20 times per mouse. It added nothing. The gain needs the mouse’s state at that moment.

  • Leave one mouse out

    Still there

    Take any one mouse away and the other two still show the gain, so no single mouse carries the result.

  • Pupil or running?

    Pupil helps every mouse

    Taking each sign away in turn: pupil helps all three mice; running helps some more than others.

Leave one mouse out

The gain stays without any one mouse.

So the pattern does not hang on one recording. It is a check, not a promise about all mice.

Average gain over the two mice that are left.

Code and data

The analysis is in AmishI123/neuroscience-project (her notebooks 01–05). The numbers on this page are copied from its saved results, and an automatic check confirms they still match. Data: Willeke et al. (2022), The Sensorium competition on predicting large-scale mouse primary visual cortex activity, arXiv:2206.08666.

What it does not show

Three mice cannot speak for all mice. A better guess does not show that being alert or running causes the brain to change. Other things that happen at the same time could explain it.
